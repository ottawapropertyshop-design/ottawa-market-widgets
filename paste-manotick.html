Manotick at a Glance

Why do buyers drive past a dozen suburbs to land in a village 25 minutes south? Because Manotick is the real article: a mill village founded by one of the Rideau’s great entrepreneurs, still organised around its 1860 mill, its river and its Main Street — and fully inside the City of Ottawa since 2001, with city services behind the village fabric. The housing runs from 19th-century village homes to some of the largest custom estates in the city’s south end. Dickinson Days, the village festival held on the first weekend of June around Dickinson’s birthday, shuts the core down for a parade, a market and fireworks — the village celebrating the man who built it. This is the prestige address of Ottawa’s rural south, and it prices like one.

The Village Core — Dickinson Square & Manotick Main Street

The core of the village is Dickinson Square: Watson’s Mill, Dickinson House and the Carriage Shed on Dickinson Street, across from the dam — described by the City of Ottawa as the city’s only industrial heritage site, and the historical centre of the village. From the square, Manotick Main Street runs its boutique course: independent shops, cafés and restaurants in heritage storefronts, the Manotick Mews shopping enclave, and the clock tower over the village grocery. This is a main street that works — daily errands, a farmers’ market in season, patios in summer — not a heritage set kept for tourists, though the mill draws tens of thousands of visitors a year.

Watson’s Mill — the Village the Mill Built

In 1858 Moss Kent Dickinson and Joseph Merrill Currier leased the water power created by a new control dam in the Rideau’s west channel at Long Island. In 1860 their limestone grist and flour mill — the Long Island Milling Enterprise — opened on the bank, five levels of locally quarried stone, its turbines cast at Currier’s Ottawa foundry. A sawmill, a carding mill, a general store and workshops followed; Dickinson laid out some thirty acres of building plots, and a village grew where the lock village of Long Island had been the only settlement. Dickinson became sole owner in 1863, built Dickinson House across the square in 1867 as the mill office, store and post office, and ran the complex alongside his Rideau forwarding fleet — the steamers and barges that made him the “King of the Rideau.” He served as Mayor of Ottawa from 1864 to 1866 and later as a Member of Parliament, and he died in Manotick in 1897.

The mill’s own story has a shadow every local knows. Ann Crosby Currier, Joseph Currier’s second wife, married in January 1861, died in the mill weeks later when her dress caught in a revolving shaft on the second floor. That much is documented. The woman said to be seen in the second-floor windows ever since is legend — the mill tells both stories honestly, and its haunted-season programming leans into the second one.

The mill passed to Alexander Spratt in the late 1920s and to Harry Watson in 1946, whose name it still carries. Commercial flour grinding ended in 1963; the Rideau Valley Conservation Authority bought and restored the square in 1972, and the mill opened as a museum in 1974. It is a working museum in the literal sense: on Sundays in season the stones still turn and grind whole-wheat flour, sold in the shop alongside bread baked from it. Mill, Dickinson House and Carriage Shed were designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act in 1979. The mill’s image is the village’s symbol — when a Manotick buyer says “the village,” this building is what they mean.

Long Island & Nicholls Island — Section 8001

West of the village core, the Rideau splits around two islands that form their own neighbourhood — Long Island and Nicholls Island (8001). Long Island is the island Dickinson named the village for: bridge-accessed, tree-canopied, and lined with waterfront homes whose backyards are the river itself — docks, launches and sunset water. Its own elementary school, St. Leonard Catholic School, stands on Long Island Road, and David Bartlett Park gives the island its green centre, with river access and the dog park. Nicholls Island, smaller and quieter, carries the same waterfront logic in a more tucked-away register. Island homes trade on water frontage and privacy; wells, septic systems and shoreline allowances are the standing homework, and the right island property rarely waits long for its buyer.

Manotick South to Roger Stevens — Section 8004

South of the village along Rideau Valley Drive, Manotick South (8004) runs down to Roger Stevens Drive — the group’s estate-lot end. This is rural-residential Manotick: large lots, custom homes, hobby-farm acreage and treed concessions, minutes from the village core and an easy run north to Highway 416. The south band is where buyers who want land with their house — room for a shop, a barn, a pool and a proper buffer of trees — do their shopping, and where the group’s largest properties sit. Servicing is the well-and-septic register here; lot homework (frontage, drainage, what can be built) decides value as much as the house does.

Manotick East & Rideau Forest — the 8005 Bank

Across the river, Manotick East (8005) runs from the east bank toward Manotick Station, and its signature community is Rideau Forest — Ottawa’s prestige estate enclave, a short passage in this hub and a full story in the Manotick East guide. Rideau Forest is a land play, not a subdivision: Rideau Forest Development Ltd., the Ken Gordon Holdings family company at 5542 Ann Street, sells serviced estate lots in phases — lots typically run an acre and a half to over two acres, with zoning in the later phases setting minimums near that range — and each buyer builds a custom home with their own builder. The vendor keeps design control: plans go to the developer for written approval before a building permit, and clearing is limited to the building envelope, which is why the community reads as one composed landscape of individual stone, stucco and brick houses in mature oak, maple and pine — “where you can’t see the homes for the trees,” as the developer puts it. Services are estate-style: underground hydro, cable, internet and natural gas, with private well and septic. Streets like Rideau Forest Drive, Queenscourt Crescent and Kilchurn Terrace carry some of the largest homes in the city’s south end, five minutes from the village — and golfers note Emerald Links is about three minutes west, across the Mitch Owens corridor in Greely (see the Greely Homes for Sale in Ottawa Neighbourhood Guide).

Living in Manotick — River, Parks & Village Life

Schools Serving Manotick

Schools are the organising spine for most Manotick families. The village holds two elementaries and a Catholic high school, with the public middle and secondary schools a short bus ride away. The standing warning applies — catchments are set by your exact home address and program, so confirm any specific property in the board’s school locator before you buy.

  • Manotick Public School (OCDSB, Junior Kindergarten–Grade 5) — 1075 Bridge Street. The village’s only public elementary, teaching in English and French, in the middle of the village it serves.

  • St. Leonard Catholic School (OCSB, JK–Grade 6) — 5344 Long Island Road. The Catholic elementary on Long Island itself — over 500 students, in English and French.

  • St. Mark Catholic High School (OCSB, Grades 7–12) — 1040 Dozois Road, in Manotick East. The Catholic secondary for the whole Manotick group, in English and French — Metcalfe’s Catholic families bus here too.

  • Kars on the Rideau Public School (OCDSB) — in Kars, south on the river. The area’s public middle school — French programming from Kindergarten and English/French through the middle grades.

  • South Carleton High School (OCDSB, Grades 9–12) — in Richmond. The public secondary for the region, drawing over 1,300 students from Manotick, Kars, North Gower and the surrounding countryside.

  • French-language boards (CEPEO / CECCE) — no French-language school sits in Manotick itself; French-board catchments follow their own sector maps, so confirm your exact address in the French boards’ locators.

Getting to Ottawa — and the Honest Transit Picture

Manotick is a driving village with a good road. Rideau Valley Drive runs north to Highway 416 in about ten minutes, and from the 416 the city opens up — downtown in roughly half an hour outside rush hour, the airport closer. River Road offers the scenic alternative through Riverside South. Transit exists but is thin: OC Transpo’s Route 186 runs a limited number of trips linking the village toward Barrhaven and the wider network — enough for some commuters, not a car replacement for most households. Buy here with two cars in mind and a commute you have test-driven at your own hour, and Manotick’s bargain — village life, city services, prestige ground — holds up honestly.

Questions Buyers Ask About Manotick

Is Manotick part of Ottawa?

Yes. Manotick is a village inside the City of Ottawa — in the rural south, in Rideau-Jock Ward — and has been since the 2001 amalgamation. It keeps its village character, its own Main Street and its K4M postal codes, but its roads, services and planning are the City of Ottawa's. Buyers get a genuine village with a city hall behind it.

How far is Manotick from downtown Ottawa?

About 25 kilometres south of downtown. Most drivers run north on Rideau Valley Drive to Highway 416, or up River Road toward Riverside South — roughly half an hour outside rush hour, longer at peak. The airport is closer still, about 15 minutes in free-flowing traffic. Test your own commute at your own hour before you buy.

What is Watson's Mill?

Watson's Mill is the limestone grist and flour mill built in 1860 by Moss Kent Dickinson and Joseph Merrill Currier on the Rideau River at Manotick — the building the village grew around, and still its symbol. Commercial flour grinding ended in 1963; it opened as a museum in 1974, and it still grinds stone-ground whole-wheat flour in demonstrations on Sundays in season, sold in the shop. With Dickinson House (1867) and the Carriage Shed it forms Dickinson Square, designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act and described by the City as Ottawa's only industrial heritage site.

Are Manotick homes on municipal water and sewer, or well and septic?

Both — and which one is the first homework on any purchase. The village core and Mahogany run on municipal water and sewer. The estate pockets — Manotick Estates' larger lots, Rideau Forest, the island and countryside properties — typically run on private wells and septic systems, with underground hydro and other services. On a well-and-septic purchase, flow, potability and septic age and capacity belong in the file before closing, not after.

What schools serve Manotick?

In and around the village: Manotick Public School (OCDSB, Junior Kindergarten to Grade 5, English and French) on Bridge Street; St. Leonard Catholic School (OCSB, JK to Grade 6) on Long Island Road; St. Mark Catholic High School (OCSB, Grades 7 to 12) on Dozois Road in Manotick East; Kars on the Rideau Public School (OCDSB) in Kars, the area's public middle school; and South Carleton High School (OCDSB, Grades 9 to 12) in Richmond, the region's public secondary. Catchments follow your exact address and program — confirm in the board's school locator before you buy.

What new homes are being built in Manotick?

The growth engine is Mahogany, Minto Communities' master-planned community south of the village core — singles, bungalows and townhomes built in phases around Mahogany Pond, parks and a protected woodlot, with the Manotick Walk pathway tying it into the village. East of the river, Rideau Forest continues its own slower model: the developer sells serviced estate lots in phases and each buyer builds a custom home under the vendor's design approval. Village infill adds a steady trickle in between.

Is there public transit in Manotick?

Limited — plan honestly around the car. OC Transpo's Route 186 runs a small number of trips linking the village toward Barrhaven and the wider network, but Manotick is a driving community: Highway 416 is minutes away, and most households run two cars. If your daily life needs frequent transit, test the 186 schedule against your routine before you fall for a house here.

Why is it called Manotick?

Moss Kent Dickinson named the village from an Anishinaabe (Algonquin/Ojibwe) word meaning 'island in the river' — for Long Island, the long island the Rideau splits around beside the mill. The name still describes the map: the village sits between the river's channels, with Long Island and Nicholls Island as its own neighbourhoods.
