If you are new to Canada and buying a home in Ottawa, the honest answer is: it depends on your status — and for most newcomers who are permanent residents, or who are working in Canada legally, the door is open today. Three separate systems decide your purchase, and they do not talk to each other: the federal foreign-buyer ban, Ontario’s Non-Resident Speculation Tax, and the mortgage rules for newcomers. You can pass one and owe money under another. This guide explains each one in plain language, with the rules as published on November 18, 2026. It is general information, not legal, immigration or tax advice — your own status always gets confirmed with a real estate lawyer before you sign anything.

Start with your status — the short answer

Everything in this guide runs off one fact: your immigration status on the day you buy. Here is the short version, status by status.

  • Permanent resident: the federal ban does not apply to you at all, and neither does the Ontario speculation tax. You buy on the same footing as a Canadian citizen, and the newcomer mortgage programs below were built for you.

  • Canadian citizen: no ban, no speculation tax, whatever your story — citizenship is the test, not where you were born or where you live.

  • Work permit holder: you are exempt from the federal ban if your permit has at least 183 days of validity left on the day you buy and you have not already bought a home under the ban’s rules — one purchase. But the Ontario tax is a separate test, and most work-permit buyers still owe it. Read both sections before you budget.

  • International student: a narrow exemption exists — five years enrolled at a designated school, five years of Canadian tax returns filed, at least 244 days a year physically in Canada, and a purchase price of $500,000 or less. Most students do not qualify; the rent-first path below is usually the honest one.

  • Protected person or refugee: exempt from the federal ban, and Ontario lists protected persons among its tax exemptions as well.

  • Not in Canada yet, or no status yet: the ban applies to you inside Ottawa until January 1, 2027, as the law stands today. Vacant land sits outside the ban. The work now is preparing — status, documents, credit file — so you can move the moment your position changes.

The federal ban — what it actually says today

The Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act has been in force since January 1, 2023. Inside Canada’s metropolitan areas — Ottawa is one — it stops people who are not citizens or permanent residents from buying houses, townhouses and condos. As the law stands today, the Act is repealed on January 1, 2027: the ban ends on that date unless Parliament changes it.

The exemptions that matter in Ottawa are the ones in the status list above: permanent residents and citizens are outside the Act entirely; a work-permit holder with 183 or more days left on the permit can buy one home (the March 2023 rule change dropped the earlier full-time-work and tax-filing conditions — what counts now is the validity left on your permit); a long-term international student can buy under the tight conditions listed; protected persons are exempt; and buying together with a spouse who is a citizen, permanent resident or otherwise exempt is also outside the ban. The ban never covered vacant land, and it does not reach homes outside the metropolitan boundary.

The Budget 2026 proposal — read this carefully. The federal budget tabled on November 5, 2026 proposes extending the ban on existing homes to January 1, 2029, while exempting newly built homes. That is a proposal, not law: the budget does not define what counts as a new home, gives no start date, and nothing has passed. Until the final rules are published, plan on the law as it stands — ban in force to January 1, 2027 — and treat any “new-build exemption” you read about as unavailable. When Ottawa’s newcomer buyers ask me what changes in 2027, that distinction is the whole answer.

The Ontario tax most newcomers miss — the NRST

Separate from the federal ban, Ontario charges a Non-Resident Speculation Tax (NRST) of 25% of the purchase price when a buyer who is not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident buys a home with one to six residences. Two facts about it surprise people. First, it applies in Ottawa: it started in 2017 as a Toronto-area tax, but since March 30, 2022 it has applied province-wide, and the rate has been 25% since October 25, 2022. Second, it does not expire with the federal ban — the two systems share nothing but a headline.

The trap is the overlap: a work-permit holder can be fully exempt from the federal ban and still owe the NRST in full. On a $700,000 Ottawa home, 25% is $175,000, payable on closing, on top of the regular land transfer tax. Ontario does run exemptions and rebates — buying jointly with a spouse who is a citizen or permanent resident, protected persons, and nominees under the Ontario Immigrant Nominee Program sit on the exemption side, and rebates exist for buyers who become permanent residents after buying and for some students and full-time workers, each with its own conditions and deadlines. Whether one fits you is a lawyer question, answered before you write an offer, not after.

The mortgage path — buying with 5% down and no Canadian credit history

Here is the part of the system built in newcomers’ favour. Through insured newcomer programs — CMHC’s Newcomers program is the best known — permanent residents and people authorized to work in Canada can buy with 5% down on a home of one or two units, and 10% down on three or four units. There is no minimum period of residency: a permanent resident who landed last month is assessed on the same footing as one who landed five years ago.

The credit question is the one newcomers worry about most, and it has a practical answer: you do not need a Canadian credit history to start. An international credit report from your home country can stand in for a Canadian bureau file, or alternative credit — twelve months of documented payment history, such as rent and utility payments made on time — can be used instead. What a lender will ask for is a document set: your status document (PR card or work permit), an employment letter, bank statements, and that payment history. Getting pre-approved before you shop is what turns those documents into a number you can house-hunt with, and a mortgage broker who works with newcomer files every week is worth having on the team from day one.

One caution that protects buyers: the first-time buyer programs — the First Home Savings Account, the Home Buyers’ Plan, Ontario’s first-time buyer land transfer tax rebate — each has its own eligibility rules, and some accounts have to be opened before you buy to do you any good. Ask about them at the pre-approval stage, not at the lawyer’s office.

The other costs, Ottawa edition

Ottawa closing costs carry one quiet advantage over Toronto: you pay Ontario land transfer tax only. There is no second, municipal land transfer tax here — that is a Toronto-only cost — and no municipal speculation tax either. Beyond the transfer tax, budget for legal fees, a home inspection, and, when your down payment is under 20%, the mortgage insurance premium that comes with an insured mortgage. None of these is Ottawa-specific except the missing second tax — but buyers arriving from Toronto research are often budgeting for a tax this city does not charge.

Rent first, or buy first?

Both sequences work, and the right one is the one your status and documents support. Buying in year one makes sense when you are a permanent resident, or a work-permit holder who clears the 183-day test and has the tax position confirmed — the newcomer mortgage programs exist precisely so that a first year in Canada does not have to be a renting year. Renting first makes sense when your status is still changing, when you want twelve months of Canadian payment history behind your mortgage file, or when you simply do not yet know which part of Ottawa fits your work and your family. Either way, the neighbourhood research is the same work: my Ottawa neighbourhood guides cover the city area by area, and for families the homes for sale by school catchment guides show which schools an address actually feeds — the second question most newcomer families ask, right after the ban.

Questions newcomers ask me

I am on a work permit. Can I buy a house in Ottawa?
Usually yes. If your work permit has at least 183 days of validity left on the day you buy and you have not already bought a home under the ban’s rules, you are exempt from the federal ban for one purchase. The separate question is Ontario’s 25% NRST, which most work-permit buyers still owe unless an exemption or rebate fits — have a lawyer confirm your tax position before you offer.

Does the 25% speculation tax really apply in Ottawa?
Yes. The NRST began as a Greater Golden Horseshoe tax in 2017, but it has applied everywhere in Ontario since March 30, 2022, at 25% since October 25, 2022. It is charged on status, not on where you live: citizens and permanent residents do not pay it; most other buyers do, unless an exemption applies.

How much down payment do I need as a newcomer?
Through insured newcomer programs, permanent residents and people authorized to work in Canada can buy with 5% down on a one- or two-unit home and 10% down on three or four units, with no minimum residency period. A Canadian credit history is not required to start — an international credit report or twelve months of documented rent and bill payments can be used instead.

What changes on January 1, 2027?
On the law as it stands today, the federal ban is repealed that day. Budget 2026, tabled November 5, 2026, proposes extending the ban on existing homes to January 1, 2029 with an exemption for newly built homes — but that is a proposal, not law, and the new-build exemption cannot be relied on until final rules are published. The Ontario NRST does not expire on January 1, 2027 either way.
