I Am Happy at My Brokerage Five Numbers Worth Checking Before You Decide to Stay

In short: If you are asking should I switch brokerages, do not decide on loyalty, habit, or a single split percentage. Check five numbers first: your effective keep rate, your total fee load, the cost of having no cap, the equity and revenue-share mechanics you are foregoing, and what it costs to switch later instead of now. If those five numbers say staying is right for you, stay — with your eyes open. If they do not, you will know exactly why.

Start With Respect for Where You Are

TL;DR: Being happy at your brokerage is a good thing. This is not a leave pitch — it is a stay-decision checklist.

Many good agents are happy where they are — they like their broker, their colleagues, and how their files are handled. None of that is in question here. A brokerage change is disruptive, even when it goes well.

But happiness does not tell you what your arrangement costs over a year. The five checks below take less than an hour with your last 12 months of statements. If they confirm you are in the right place, you will stay with more confidence, not less. For the eXp Canada model in full, see the hub at https://www.sellingottawa.realestate/blog/Fed+Up+With+Your+Brokerage+How+eXp+Realty+Actually+Works.

Number One: Your Effective Keep Rate

TL;DR: Divide what you actually kept by your gross commission income. That percentage — not your headline split — is your real split.

Your headline split is the number on your agreement. Your effective keep rate is what actually happened. Take your gross commission income (GCI) for the last 12 months, subtract every dollar your brokerage kept or charged, and divide what landed in your account by your GCI.

Agents are often surprised by the gap once monthly and per-deal fees are included. For comparison, the posted eXp Canada structure is 80/20 up to a $16,000 CAD cap at $80,000 GCI, then $285 per deal for 20 deals (maximum $5,700), then $75 per deal, plus a $139 monthly cloud fee, a $199 startup fee, and $29 per deal for broker review. Whether it beats yours depends entirely on your effective rate.

What split am I really on if I have never calculated it?

Your effective keep rate. That single percentage is the only fair basis for comparing any two brokerages, including staying where you are.

Number Two: Your Total Fee Load

TL;DR: List every recurring and per-deal charge separately. Fees that feel small monthly can be the largest line annually.

Your split is only one part of the cost. Go through a full year of statements and list each category on its own line:

  • Monthly desk, office, or franchise fees, multiplied by 12
  • Technology, CRM, website, and marketing fees
  • Per-deal transaction, administration, and broker review fees
  • Errors and omissions insurance and board or association pass-through charges billed by the brokerage
  • Referral fees, mentorship splits, and any royalty or franchise percentage taken on top of your split
  • One-time and annual fees: startup, renewal, training, and event charges you are required to pay

Total them. Some lines will be worth it — good support is worth paying for. Others survive only because they have always been there. Knowing which is which is the point.

Number Three: The Cost of Having No Cap

TL;DR: Without a cap, every additional deal costs you the same percentage forever. With a cap, your marginal cost per deal falls sharply after a set point.

A cap is a ceiling on the total split dollars a brokerage takes in your anniversary year. At eXp in Canada, that ceiling is $16,000 CAD, reached at $80,000 GCI on the 80/20 split. After the cap, the per-deal cost drops to the $285 and then $75 schedule described above, and the cap resets on your anniversary date.

If you have no cap, apply your split to your full 12-month GCI and compare it with the same production under a capped structure. The difference grows with production. If your production is modest, it may be small — useful information too.

Does a cap matter if I am not a top producer?

Less than for a high producer — it is honest to say so. Below the cap threshold, monthly fees and support may matter more. At or above $80,000 GCI, the cap is usually the largest number in the comparison.

Number Four: Equity and Revenue-Share Mechanics You Are Foregoing

TL;DR: Some models include stock and revenue-share mechanics; traditional splits usually do not. Count the mechanics for what they are — not as promised income.

This number is different from the first three because it is not a cost on your statement. It is a structure you either have access to or you do not. Revenue share at eXp is explained in full in the explainer at https://www.sellingottawa.realestate/blog/eXp+Realty+Revenue+Share+Explained+The+7+Tier+System — in brief, it is a share of company revenue paid on the production of agents you sponsor, not a charge taken from those agents. Amounts vary and are not guaranteed — see eXp's income disclosure.

Value it at zero if you want to — many agents do. Do not project it or accept a forecast. The only question is whether, over a 10- or 20-year career, having access to the mechanism matters to you at all. Your answer can legitimately be no — it should be an answer, not an oversight.

Number Five: The Cost of Switching Later Versus Now

TL;DR: Switching has a one-time cost in time and attention. Delay also has a cost — another year under a structure you have already measured.

Every switch involves friction: new systems, paperwork, informing clients, rebranding, and a learning curve — roughly the same whenever you move. What changes with timing is how many more years you spend under the old cost structure, and where a move falls relative to your cap anniversary and busy season.

Use this simple stay-decision checklist before you decide either way:

  • My effective keep rate for the last 12 months is calculated, not assumed
  • My total annual fee load is listed line by line
  • I know whether I have a cap, and what having or lacking one cost me last year
  • I have considered equity and revenue-share mechanics at a value I chose myself, including zero
  • I have weighed the one-time cost of switching against the recurring cost of another year as-is
  • My decision to stay or move is based on these numbers — and I could explain it to my family in five minutes

If you can tick every box and staying wins, stay, and re-run the numbers once a year. If several boxes point the other way, have an informed conversation before your next anniversary, not after it.
