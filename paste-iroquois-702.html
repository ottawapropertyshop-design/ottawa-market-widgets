A Short History — Point Iroquois to the Town That Moved

The settlement begins at the point. Point Iroquois, a wooded headland jutting into the St. Lawrence, drew Indigenous camps long before it drew settlers, and the village that grew beside it after the Loyalist arrivals of the 1780s took its name from the point. The river here ran the Galop Rapids, and the village’s first century ran on bypassing them: the Iroquois Canal, part of the Williamsburg Canals, locked river boats past the rapids, and the old lock can still be seen at the end of Carman Road, on the way to the new lock’s lookout. The village incorporated in 1857 — the first in the area to have water services — and the stone Carman House, named for one of the original Loyalist families, stands as its museum piece.

Then came the project that remade the whole shore. The St. Lawrence Seaway needed a new lock and a control dam at Point Iroquois, and Lake St. Lawrence would rise over the old waterfront village. So Iroquois did what almost no town has ever done: it moved. In the 1950s the village was relocated about a kilometre and a half north, out of the flood area, and laid out as a planned town on the high ground — while Morrisburg, twelve kilometres east, was half-flooded and rebuilt in place, and ten villages further east were lost entirely. On January 1, 1998, Iroquois and Morrisburg amalgamated with Matilda and Williamsburgh Townships into the Municipality of South Dundas. Iroquois gave up its separate council and kept everything that mattered: the lock, the high school, the river and the name.

Servicing — the Iroquois Homework

Iroquois is a serviced town wrapped in a rural township, and the line between the two is a lot line, not a guess. Before any purchase here:

  • Confirm the services for the exact lot with the Municipality of South Dundas — whether the property connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private system, and what connection charges apply. Never assume from the street name.

  • On a well and septic property — the norm just outside the town edge — budget for a potability and bacteria water test, a flow check, and a septic inspection with the tank pumped and the bed assessed. A replacement bed is one of the largest cheques a rural buyer can inherit.

  • Heating history matters — oil, propane and electric heat are all common out here, and each carries a very different monthly reality through a St. Lawrence winter.

  • For postwar homes, look hard at foundation, wiring and insulation before conditions come off — the planned town’s 1950s stock is honest, and its systems are the age they are.

  • On riverfront lots, ask about shoreline and flood history specifically — this shore was remade by a flood once, on purpose, and shoreline allowances are standard questions on any shore-side property.

None of this is a reason to avoid Iroquois — half the Seaway shore lives happily on some version of it. It is a reason to buy with your eyes open, and it is standard practice in every offer I write out here.

Schools — the Whole Path Inside One Town

This is the fact that organizes everything else about Iroquois, and the reason this ring of guides is built the way it is: if you buy a home here, your younger child starts at Iroquois Public School and your older one finishes at Seaway District High School — and both schools are inside the same town. Iroquois is the Seaway’s home town: the high school that serves the whole South Dundas hub — Morrisburg, Matilda and Williamsburgh included — sits here, so the bus rides of this hub end at Iroquois’ door.

  • Iroquois Public School (Upper Canada District School Board), 66 Lakeview Drive — Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6, English and French Immersion both offered. One school, both official-language tracks, for every child in town.

  • Seaway District High School (UCDSB), 2 Beach Street — Grades 7 to 12, about 400 students. The school opened in 1957 in the rebuilt town, and Morrisburg’s high-school students joined it in 1967 when Morrisburg Collegiate closed — the two towns have shared this one high school ever since, and it stands in Iroquois.

  • Catholic: the Catholic elementary school for this shore is St. Mary-St. Cecilia Catholic School, in Morrisburg (CDSBEO) — it is not in Iroquois, and no Catholic school sits inside this district. Catholic secondary assignment for Iroquois addresses is address-specific: confirm it with the CDSBEO locator directly rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

  • French-language boards: assignments for Iroquois addresses are address-specific — run your exact address through the French boards’ locators rather than relying on any summary, including this one.

Read the path the way a family lives it: the same high school serves the whole hub — Morrisburg’s children ride here from the hub town, Matilda’s and Williamsburgh’s come in from the township rings — while Iroquois’ own students are already home. Questions Buyers Ask About Iroquois

Is Iroquois, Ontario a good place to live?
For the right buyer, it is one of the most distinctive small towns in Ontario. You get a planned town laid out fresh in the 1950s, the St. Lawrence River and the Seaway lock at the foot of town, a golf course, and something almost no town its size can offer: the whole public school path inside the town limits, elementary and high school both. The honest trade-offs: bigger shopping and the hospital are drives, there is no public transit, and daily life runs on a car. Buyers who choose Iroquois are usually choosing the river, the ships and the value on purpose — and a town that was moved once, on purpose, and rebuilt itself tends to keep that character.

Which schools serve Iroquois?
The whole public path sits inside the town. Iroquois Public School (Upper Canada District School Board, 66 Lakeview Drive) takes children from Junior Kindergarten to Grade 6, in English and French Immersion. Seaway District High School (UCDSB, 2 Beach Street) takes Grades 7 to 12 — the same high school Morrisburg’s teenagers travel to, except Iroquois’ students are already here. Catholic elementary for this shore is St. Mary-St. Cecilia Catholic School in Morrisburg, twelve kilometres east; Catholic secondary assignment and French-language school assignment are address-specific, so confirm them with the boards directly. And as always: confirm the schools assigned to an exact address with the board before you buy.

How far is Iroquois from Ottawa?
About 71 kilometres straight-line from downtown — roughly an hour and a quarter by road in normal conditions. The run is simple: Highway 401 from the Iroquois interchange to Highway 416, then straight north into the city. Morrisburg is twelve kilometres east along the river, Prescott about 35 minutes west, and Cornwall about half an hour east. Winter adds its opinion, as it does everywhere along the river, but there is no route-finding in this commute — it is two highways, both of them straight.

What is the difference between Iroquois and Morrisburg?
They are sister towns twelve kilometres apart, rebuilt in the same Seaway decade, and buyers cross-shop them constantly. Morrisburg is the bigger town: the main commercial centre of South Dundas, its own elementary school and the Catholic school, the plaza and the waterfront. Iroquois is the planned town: moved whole about a kilometre and a half north in the 1950s and laid out fresh, it holds the Seaway lock and control dam, its own elementary school, and the high school both towns share — Seaway District High School. Morrisburg sells town services; Iroquois sells the high school at the door and the ships passing the lock. The Morrisburg hub guide on this site covers both towns and the township rings around them.

Why was Iroquois moved?
The St. Lawrence Seaway moved it. The new shipping channel, the Iroquois Lock and the control dam that regulates Lake Ontario’s outflow all had to be built at Point Iroquois, and the rising Lake St. Lawrence would have flooded the old waterfront village. So in the 1950s the village was moved about a kilometre and a half north to higher ground and laid out as a planned town — new streets, new lots, houses moved or rebuilt — before the water came in 1958. The old village site, the old canal and the Galop Rapids it bypassed are part of the shore’s story now; the town that stands today is the replacement, and it wears that history openly.

What is the Iroquois Lock?
The westernmost lock of the St. Lawrence Seaway’s river section, at Point Iroquois at the south edge of town. Ocean-going ships and Great Lakes freighters pass through it on their way between the Atlantic and Lake Ontario, regulated by the control dam across the river — the structure that manages the outflow of Lake Ontario itself. For residents it is the town’s front-row seat: ship-watching from the shore is a local pastime, and the lock is the reason the town stands where it stands. Almost every other place on this shore watches the river; Iroquois works it.

Are homes in Iroquois on municipal water and sewer?
Iroquois is a serviced town, but ask the question lot by lot — that is the honest answer, and it is one of the first checks on any purchase here. The town core connects to municipal services; properties at the town edge and on the concession roads just outside the district can be serviced very differently. Before you buy, confirm with the Municipality of South Dundas whether the exact lot connects to municipal water and sewer or runs on a private well and septic system, and budget the inspections accordingly — a water test and a septic inspection are standard practice on rural-fringe offers.
