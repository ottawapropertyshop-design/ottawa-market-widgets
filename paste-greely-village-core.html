The Village Core story — Greely’s original centre

Greely began here. Long before the lake communities and estate subdivisions that fill the rest of this series, the village was a farm crossroads where Bank Street — the old Prescott highway — met the roads west to Manotick and south through the township. The streets that carry that first village are still the core’s skeleton: Meadow Drive running east from Parkway Road to Old Prescott Road, the small grid of Johnston Drive, D’Arcy Street, Cuthbert Way and Cosgrove Avenue west of Parkway, and the lanes — Terry Street, Pig Alley, Good Street — that remember a working farm village. The lots tell the same story: smaller than the estate parcels around them, the kind of 100-by-150-foot village lots homes were built on before the subdivision era.

Bank Street is the Greely core’s commercial spine, and it still does the village’s daily work: The Shoppes of Greely — the Foodland plaza — anchors it, with the services, shops and take-out counters a village of this size runs on. Meadow Drive carries the civic side of the core: the Greely Community Centre at 1448 Meadow Drive, with the Ottawa Public Library’s Greely branch beside it, is the village’s living room — the Winter Carnival, the minor hockey and soccer registrations, the community events that make Greely a village and not just a postal code. Greely Elementary School stands on Parkway Road at the core’s west edge, and the Parkway churches have watched over the crossroads for generations.

Churches in Greely — the Village Core’s three churches

Three churches anchor the Village Core’s Sundays. Two stand on Parkway Road in Greely, one toward each end of the core — at the Meadow Drive end, the small white church built in 1889; at the Bank Street end, the big brick church of the village’s growth years. The third is the oldest parish of the three: St. John the Evangelist at Enniskerry, the Catholic church Greely families have belonged to since before there was a village. All three are living congregations, not landmarks — services run every Sunday in each.

All Saints Anglican Church — 7103 Parkway Road, Greely


Photo: Tomasz R. Witek — All Saints Anglican Church, 7103 Parkway Road, with the cemetery beside it.

Greely’s Anglicans were a congregation before they were a building: from 1865 to 1889 they belonged to the Deanery of Prescott-Russell and were served by the Rev. W. Fleming, a mission priest. In 1889, during the incumbency of the Rev. J.B. Haslam, the congregation built the white clapboard church that still stands on Parkway Road, on land donated by Richard and Frances Stanley, and the Rt. Rev. Charles Hamilton consecrated it. The name is the dedication itself — the church was dedicated, in its own words, in honour of those saintly persons who have no special day in the year.

The building is a village church at true village scale: a nave of 635 square feet with ten stained-glass windows and sixteen benches seating about 75, and a 575-square-foot hall beside it. Richard and Frances Stanley lie in the cemetery next to the church, with many of their descendants — a roll call of the families who farmed this village into being. The congregation still gathers every Sunday at 9:00 a.m.; its annual cemetery service draws former parishioners back from across the province, and a memorial garden was added in 2013. All Saints is a parish of the Anglican Diocese of Ottawa.

Parkway Church — 7275 Parkway Road, Greely


Photo: Tomasz R. Witek — Parkway Church, 7275 Parkway Road, at the Bank Street corner.

At the north end of the core, where Parkway Road meets Bank Street, stands Greely’s Pentecostal church — Parkway Road Pentecostal Church, a congregation of the Pentecostal Assemblies of Canada that today worships simply as Parkway Church. The congregation was already at this corner by the mid-1990s: a church directory in the Winchester Press of June 1995 lists Parkway Road Pentecostal at the corner of Highway 31 and Parkway Road. It has grown with the village around it ever since.

The building tells that growth story. The large brick church in the photograph, with its covered entrance and steeple, is the product of a planned expansion — in the mid-2010s the congregation was working with Hawkey Church Management, a firm that builds churches, on the phased build-out of its master plan. Today Parkway is one of the village’s biggest gathering places: Sunday services at 10:30 a.m. led by Lead Pastor Nathaniel Venasse, who joined the staff in 2015 and became lead pastor in July 2020, kids’ and youth programs through the week, and community events — the big Eastern Ontario Christmas craft market among them — filling the building through the year.

St. John the Evangelist — Stagecoach Road at Enniskerry, Greely


Photo: Tomasz R. Witek — St. John the Evangelist, Enniskerry, with the cemetery beside it.

South of the village, on Stagecoach Road at Enniskerry, stands the Village Core’s Catholic parish church: St. John the Evangelist, a Roman Catholic parish that dates its founding to 1854. It began as a mission served from Our Lady of the Visitation at South Gloucester, and its first resident priest, Fr. Thomas O’Boyle — born in County Mayo, and pastor at the Visitation before he came here — took charge in 1860, with St. Brigid’s in Manotick as his mission. Greely never built a Catholic church inside the village; for its Catholic families, St. John’s has been the parish church all along.

Fr. O’Boyle died in 1866 and lies in the cemetery beside his church, among the Daleys, Dewans, McEvoys, Doyles and Herberts whose farms filled the pews. The parish endured fires and rebuilt; it ran a temperance hall in the settlement then called Dawson; and between 1978 and 1984 it planted a daughter church of its own, building St. Leonard’s in Manotick as its mission. The pairing today runs the other way from 1860: St. John’s is twinned with St. Brigid’s, the parish office sits on River Road in Manotick, and the two congregations share one pastoral team.
