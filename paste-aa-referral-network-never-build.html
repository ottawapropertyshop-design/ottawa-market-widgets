Why Most Referrals Happen by Accident

TL;DR: A favour from a friend is not a network. A network is a short list of agents you already know, in places your clients actually move to, with the paperwork agreed before a name changes hands.

Think about the last client who told you they were moving. A job transfer to Alberta. A parent downsizing to be near family in British Columbia. Newcomers arriving in Ottawa from overseas, with a home still to sell back home. In each case, there were two transactions — and most agents only ever see one of them.

The outgoing half usually leaks away. The agent searches online, picks a name from a directory, makes one hopeful phone call, and never hears what happened. There is no agreement in writing, no update at closing, and no relationship to use the next time. That is not a referral business. That is a coin toss with your client's move riding on it.

A built network works the other way around. You choose your partners before a client needs them. You know how they communicate, how fast they respond, and which neighbourhoods and price ranges they actually work. When the call comes, you are not searching. You are introducing.

How Referral Fees and Referral Structure Actually Work

TL;DR: A referral is a written agreement between brokerages, made before the introduction. The referring agent is paid an agreed share of the receiving agent's commission, by the brokerages, after the deal closes and is paid.

Strip away the jargon and a referral has four steps:

  • Agreement first. Before any client name is shared, the two agents agree the terms in writing, on a referral agreement signed through their brokerages. No agreement, no referral — this protects the client and both agents.
  • Introduction second. You introduce the client directly, explain why you chose this agent, and stay in the loop. A warm introduction converts. A forwarded phone number usually does not.
  • The receiving agent does the work. They serve the client, negotiate the deal, and earn the commission in their own market, under their own licence and their own brokerage split.
  • Payment at closing. The agreed referral fee is calculated as a percentage of the receiving side's gross commission and is paid brokerage to brokerage once the transaction closes and the commission is received. If the deal does not close, there is no commission — and no referral fee.

There is no single standard percentage that applies to every referral, every market, and every price range — the rate is negotiated case by case and written into the agreement. Build the Network Before You Need It

TL;DR: Pick the markets your clients actually move to and from, vet a small number of agents in each, agree your terms in advance, and keep a simple record of every introduction and outcome.

A practical build takes weeks, not months:

  • Map your real corridors. Look at your last three years of clients. Where did they come from, and where did they go? For most Ottawa agents the list is short: Toronto and the GTA, Alberta, British Columbia, the Ottawa Valley — and, for agents serving newcomer communities, the countries those families came from.
  • Vet for response time first. Production matters, but a referral lives or dies on communication. Call the agent yourself. If your call takes two days to come back, so will your client's.
  • Agree terms while nothing is at stake. Referral percentage, update rhythm, and what happens if the client does not close — settled calmly in advance, not negotiated over a live family move.
  • Track every introduction. Date, client, market, receiving agent, outcome. That record is the difference between hoping referrals grow and knowing whether they did.
  • Feed the network both ways. Send market news, answer questions about Ottawa, and be the agent other agents think of when their client says the word Ottawa. Networks that only take, stop receiving.

Can I refer a client to an agent in another country if I am not licensed there?

Yes — that is exactly what a referral is. You do not act as the client's agent in that market and you do not give advice under a licence you do not hold. You introduce the client to a locally licensed agent, under a written referral agreement between the brokerages, and that agent does the work. Your role is the trusted introduction and the follow-up.

What should I check before I refer a client to an agent I have never worked with?

Three things, in this order: that the agent is licensed and active in the exact market your client is moving to; that they respond to you the way you would want them to respond to your client; and that the referral terms are agreed in writing before the introduction is made. If any one of the three fails, keep looking — your client will remember your name on that move, not theirs.

The Quiet Income Line on Your Business

TL;DR: Referrals will rarely be your biggest income line, and they should never be promised as one. What they reliably do is turn moves you were going to lose anyway into served clients, returned goodwill, and a second transaction you would never have seen.

Be honest about what a referral network is. It is not passive income and it is not guaranteed income. Clients change plans, deals fall through, and some introductions go nowhere. Any agent who promises you a referral fortune is selling you something.

What the network does do, year after year, is compound trust. The family you guided through both ends of an international move does not shop for an agent next time. The agent in another province who received three clean, well-prepared introductions from you sends the fourth Ottawa-bound client your way without being asked.
