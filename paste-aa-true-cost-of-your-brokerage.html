The True Cost of Your Brokerage A Canadian Fee Audit You Can Run Tonight

The true cost of your brokerage is not your split. It is every dollar that leaves your commission before you see it — split, royalty, desk fees, tech fees, transaction fees, review fees, insurance, marketing and referral fees combined — divided by what you actually earned. This post gives you a line-by-line audit you can run tonight on your own last 12 months, using your own statements, so you can compare any brokerage on total cost instead of on a headline split.

Why Your Split Is the Wrong Number to Compare

TL;DR: An 80/20 split can cost more than a 70/30 split once monthly fees, per-deal fees and royalties are added — the only honest comparison is total dollars paid in a full year.

Most agents choose a brokerage by split because it is the one number in the recruitment conversation. It is also the easiest number to make look good. A brokerage can offer an attractive split and recover the difference in a monthly desk fee, a technology fee, a franchise royalty, a per-transaction charge, a broker review fee and a marketing levy you barely notice because each one is small and each one arrives at a different time.

The audit in this post fixes that. You are not guessing, and you are not using anyone's brochure numbers. You are using your own commission statements from the last 12 months — the record of what your business actually cost to run through your current brokerage. Once you have that total, every other brokerage conversation changes. You stop asking "what is your split?" and start asking "what would my last 12 months have cost me there?"

Step One: Gather the Right Paperwork

TL;DR: Pull 12 months of commission statements, your monthly brokerage invoices, and your independent contractor agreement — the agreement shows the fees your statements may not itemise.

You need three sources, because no single document shows the whole picture:

  • Your commission statements or deal sheets for every closed transaction in the last 12 months
  • Your monthly brokerage invoices or credit card statements showing recurring fees
  • Your current independent contractor agreement, which lists fees that may be charged even in months when you close nothing

Work in gross commission income (GCI) — the total commission your deals generated before any split. Write that number down first. Every percentage in this audit is measured against it, and it is the number that turns a vague feeling of "my fees seem high" into a figure you can act on.

Step Two: The Line-by-Line Audit

TL;DR: Work through nine lines — split, royalty, desk, tech, transaction, review, E&O, marketing and referral fees — total them, then divide by your GCI for your true effective rate.

Go line by line, and write a real dollar total for the full 12 months beside each one:

  • Commission split: the brokerage's share of every deal — usually the biggest line, so calculate it from GCI, not from memory
  • Royalty or franchise fee: a percentage off the top at many traditional brands, charged whether or not you use the brand's tools
  • Desk fee: a monthly charge, whether you use a physical desk or not — multiply by 12
  • Technology fee: monthly platform, CRM, website or email charges, including "optional" tools you are billed for anyway
  • Transaction fee: a flat charge per closed deal — multiply by your number of deals
  • Broker review or compliance fee: the per-file charge for having your paperwork reviewed
  • Errors and omissions insurance: annual or per-deal E&O charges passed through by the brokerage
  • Marketing and brand fees: sign, lockbox, advertising fund or listing syndication charges
  • Referral fees paid through the brokerage: relocation or lead-program referrals where the brokerage takes a share before your split

Now add every line. That is your total brokerage cost for the year. Divide it by your GCI and multiply by 100 — that percentage is your true effective rate. If you are on an 80/20 split but your effective rate comes out at 32 percent, the missing 12 points are the fees nobody put in the recruitment pitch.

Red Flags the Audit Usually Exposes

TL;DR: Fees that rise when you earn more, fees for services you never use, and fees you cannot find in your own agreement are the three patterns worth acting on.

Most agents who run this audit find at least one of these:

  • No cap, or a cap that keeps moving: your split cost grows forever with your production, with no point where your keep rate improves
  • Paying for an office you do not use: desk and facility fees for a cloud-based work pattern
  • Stacked per-deal fees: transaction, review, compliance and E&O charges that quietly add hundreds of dollars to every closing
  • Fees missing from your agreement: charges on your statements that you cannot match to a clause you signed — ask your broker of record to point each one out, in writing
  • Exit friction: clauses about pending deals, listing ownership or tail fees that would follow you if you left — know these before you need them, not after

Is it worth switching brokerages just to save on fees?

Fees alone are not the whole decision — support, training, lead generation and fit matter, and a move has real disruption cost. What the audit gives you is the size of the fee question. If the gap is a few hundred dollars a year, it may not be worth a move. If it is five figures a year, every year, that is money that could fund your marketing, your retirement savings or your family — and it deserves a serious conversation.
