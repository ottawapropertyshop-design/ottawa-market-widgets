When to close your pool in Ottawa

If your pool is still open, you are inside the last safe window of the year — not past it, but close. HB Pools, the Ottawa company that publishes its booking window, takes closings from September 10 to October 20, and adds a $100 fee for closings left until after the first week of November. Puddle Pools, also Ottawa, gives the rule behind the dates: close once the water stays consistently below 15°C, which in Ottawa typically happens between late September and mid-October. Ottawa’s average first frost is October 12. A closing done while the water is still warm wastes chemicals and grows algae under the cover; a closing left until the lines freeze cannot be done at all. Close late — but before freeze-up.

A short history of the backyard pool

People have been building pools for a very long time. The earliest known large man-made bathing pool is the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro, in present-day Pakistan, dated to roughly 2600 BCE — the date varies by source, so treat it as approximate; scholars believe it served ritual purification rather than swimming. In Rome, Gaius Maecenas is credited with the first heated pool in the 1st century BCE. The modern public-pool era starts with legislation: Britain’s Public Baths and Wash-houses Act of 1846 let towns fund public baths and pools from public money, in the middle of a cholera-driven public-health movement. Olympic swimming began in open water in 1896 — the first purpose-built Olympic pool came in London in 1908, a 100-metre pool built inside White City Stadium, unheated and unchlorinated. The backyard pool is a post-war invention: rising prosperity and falling construction costs produced the 1950s–60s North American boom, and in Canada the pool landed in middle-class suburban backyards after the Second World War. No source gives a specific date for pools arriving in Ottawa suburbs — anyone who tells you one is guessing — but by the time Barrhaven, Kanata and the estate villages filled in, the inground pool was a standard feature of the big Ottawa lot. And every one of them, every fall, has to be closed.

The three pool types — and why each closes differently

Inground pools (vinyl liner, fibreglass, concrete/gunite) share one closing sequence; what changes is how far the water is lowered and what the shell cannot tolerate. Vinyl: traditionally about 6 inches below the skimmer mouth — and never drained empty, because the water holds the liner in place and stops it shrinking and shifting. Concrete: about 12 inches below the skimmer mouth, or just below the returns — the tile line is freeze-vulnerable. Fibreglass: follow the manufacturer’s level; Ottawa’s HB Pools runs a duck-plug system on fibreglass that doesn’t lower the water at all. On every shell, a fully drained pool can be lifted by groundwater pressure — the hydrostatic “pop” is the most expensive mistake in this guide.

Above-ground pools close lighter: there are usually no buried lines to blow out — Hayward Canada makes exactly this point. The kit is an air pillow centred under the cover, inflated only about half full so the sheet of ice squeezes the pillow instead of pushing on the walls; a skimmer plate or plug so the water doesn’t have to drop as far; and a cover held by a cable-and-winch around the top rail — never water bags on the rails. Pump, filter and hoses are drained and usually stored indoors.

Indoor pools are not closed. No draining, no line blowing, no cover — the building keeps everything above freezing and the pool stays swimmable all winter. What changes is the building, not the pool: humidity control becomes the whole game (the UK pool standards body SPATA puts pool-hall humidity at a maximum of 55–65%, to stop condensation rotting the structure), heating costs rise because cold outside air has to be warmed, and the pool hall is kept at negative pressure so chlorinated air doesn’t migrate into the house. An indoor pool taken out of service for months is an HVAC mothballing job, not a pool-closing service — and it isn’t something pool companies sell as one.

Hot tub winterization in Ottawa — drain it or keep it running

For a hot tub, winter is not the off-season — it’s the best season, and the manufacturers’ default advice is to keep the tub filled, heated and circulating if you’ll use it at all. Heated, circulating water doesn’t freeze. Royal Spa’s guidance is to drain only if you’ll be away three months or more. The risks of running are real but specific: a multi-day power outage nobody notices, or a water level that drops and lets the pump run dry.

A proper dry winterization — the route if the tub will sit unused — follows Canadian manufacturer Hydropool’s manual: power off, drain completely, remove and clean the filter, vacuum every jet and the filter housing dry with a wet/dry vac, add potable RV antifreeze (propylene glycol — never automotive antifreeze) to the pump wet end, jet lines and filter housing, disconnect the unions, pull the lowest pump drain plug, cover the tub and keep snow cleared off it. The risks run the other way too: miss one pocket of water in a jet line and it cracks; seals and gaskets left dry all winter can dry out, crack and leak at refill. And freeze damage in a winterized tub is not covered by the manufacturer’s warranty — which is why most Ottawa owners who soak all winter simply keep the water hot.

Balance the water before you close

A closed pool is a sealed system for six months — whatever state the water is in on closing day is the state it works on your liner, plaster and equipment with, all winter, with no pump running to forgive mistakes. So the chemistry pass happens a few days before closing day, not the morning of. Test first, and test properly: a liquid drop test kit is the accurate home method, test strips are a quick cross-check, and the most complete read is a sample bottle taken to a pool store’s water lab — most Ottawa pool stores run computerized water testing in-store (the retail list at the end of this guide says which ones advertise it). Test a few days ahead so there is time to adjust, retest, and still close on schedule.

Adjust in this order: alkalinity, then pH, then calcium. Total alkalinity first — 80–120 ppm — because alkalinity is the anchor: it buffers the water so pH can’t drift wildly over the winter. Then pH, 7.2–7.8: run low and the water turns corrosive and etches plaster, grout and metal fittings for six straight months; run high and it lays down scale and goes cloudy. Then calcium hardness, 180–220 ppm on Hayward Canada’s closing guidance: soft water satisfies itself by pulling calcium out of plaster, concrete and grout, while very hard water deposits scale on surfaces and heaters. Vinyl liners don’t give up calcium the way plaster does, but high calcium still scales any surface — and fibreglass owners should follow their shell maker’s range.

Sanitizer and stabilizer. Two or three days before closing, shock the pool — calcium hypochlorite is the standard chlorine shock; non-chlorine oxidizer shock is the other type, and it’s what some winterizing kits use. Then let the chlorine level fall back to the 1–3 ppm range before any algaecide goes in — shock and algaecide are never added together, because high chlorine destroys the algaecide before it can work (Hayward Canada’s sequence, and the reason step order matters). Stabilizer (cyanuric acid, CYA) is the sunscreen that keeps sun from burning chlorine off in summer; Taylor Technologies’ guidance puts the right level for an outdoor chlorine pool at 30–50 ppm. Closing is not the time to add it — too much CYA ties chlorine up and makes it sluggish, and CYA doesn’t break down over winter, it just sits there. If yours ran high all summer, it will still be high in May; the fix is partial water replacement, not more chemicals. Salt-water pools run higher CYA in season (roughly 60–80 ppm) to suit the generator — same rule applies: test it, don’t add to it in October.

The winter-specific chemicals. On top of balanced water, a proper closing adds four specialists. A winterizing algaecide — the long-lasting poly-60 (polyquat) type is the one pros reach for — goes in last among the water chemicals, at the label rate, after the chlorine has fallen, and gets circulated so it reaches every corner; it is the insurance policy that keeps the water from going green under the cover by March. A stain and scale preventer (a metal sequestrant) locks onto iron and copper in the water so they can’t plate out as stains on the liner or steps over winter — it matters most if your fill water is a rural well, where iron is common. An enzyme treatment (closing formulas are sold for exactly this) keeps breaking down the oils, sunscreen and organic residue in the water all winter, which is what stops the black waterline ring you’d otherwise scrub off in May. And some kits add a slow-release winter floater or cartridge that dispenses a trickle of winter chemical until freeze-up — optional, label-directed, and only ever a product made for winter use. The fifth chemical isn’t for the pool water at all: pool antifreeze goes in the plumbing lines (step 7 below).

Salt pools: the salt cell gets cleaned — descaled — before it comes off the line and is stored indoors for the winter, and the generator is shut down for the season. Two Ottawa firms publish the job as a priced line item (GBI at $35, Purewater at $49.99, directory below), which tells you it’s a step, not an upsell.

The closing-chemicals shopping list (for the DIY version): a winterizing kit sized to your pool — kits bundle the shock, winter algaecide and stain & scale preventer, and are usually the economical route; two Ottawa-published prices anchor the range: Purewater sells its closing chemical kit at $49.99, and GBI’s closing chemical kit is a $56 add-on on a booked closing — or the same three products bought separately. Plus: a test kit or fresh strips; non-toxic pool antifreeze, roughly a bottle per line (HB Pools publishes $14.95 +HST a bottle); an air pillow for an above-ground pool; and water bags or cover clips if you run a tarp cover. Everything on that list is stock at the stores in Part B of the directory below.

Skip the balance and the winter sends the bill: algae blooms quietly under the cover and you open to a green swamp in May; scale builds a rough line at the waterline; dissolved metals stain liners and steps; soft, acidic water etches plaster; and excess chlorine left in at closing fades a liner by spring. Every one of those is a spring expense that a $50 kit and an hour of testing in October prevents.

How to close an inground pool, step by step

This is the sequence the industry teaches — Hayward Canada’s order — and it’s what a professional crew is working through at your house:

  1. Balance the water — the full chemistry pass in the section above, done a few days ahead: alkalinity 80–120 ppm, pH 7.2–7.8, calcium hardness 180–220 ppm, shock two or three days out and the winter algaecide only after the chlorine falls back to 1–3 ppm. Unbalanced water sits against the liner and equipment for six months.
  2. Clean — brush the walls, vacuum, skim every leaf out. Organic matter left in the pool consumes chlorine and stains.
  3. Closing chemicals — shock per the package directions, then let the chlorine fall back to 1.0–3.0 ppm before adding the winter algaecide. Never add shock and algaecide at the same time. Winterizing kits add a stain and scale preventer.
  4. Lower the water — by cover type. Mesh safety cover: 12–18 inches below the skimmer. Solid floating cover: 3–6 inches below the tile line. Never drain the pool empty (liner support and hydrostatic pressure, above).
  5. Drain the equipment pad — open the drain plugs on pump, filter, heater and chlorinator. Sand filter: backwash and set the multiport to winter. DE: flush and clean the grids. Cartridge: remove, clean, dry, store indoors. Take the pressure gauge off and store it inside, and put every drain plug in the pump basket so they can be found in May. Remove the salt cell and store it indoors.
  6. Blow out the lines — from the skimmer through the equipment and back to the pool, plugging each line as it clears. The main drain can’t be plugged: blow it until it bubbles, then close its valve to trap an air lock.
  7. Add pool antifreeze — non-toxic propylene-glycol pool antifreeze only, never automotive antifreeze, into the lines and skimmer. Protect the skimmer with a Gizzmo or expansion plug — the sacrificial part that absorbs ice expansion so the skimmer body doesn’t crack.
  8. Remove and store the accessories — ladders, rails, baskets, return fittings, cleaner, solar blanket — indoors. Lubricate the O-rings.
  9. Cover. A solid tarp cover blocks all light (the best algae suppression) and is held by water bags — but meltwater pools on top and needs a cover pump. A mesh safety cover is anchored to the deck with straps, springs and recessed anchors; water drains through it, so no pump, and it typically outlasts a tarp — but it lets in fine silt and some light. HB Pools no longer installs tarp covers at all, citing safety — a sign of where the industry has gone.

Ottawa rules for the water you pump out. The closing companies state Ottawa’s discharge rules on their own booking pages: non-salt pool water is dechlorinated before discharge, so the wastewater is chlorine-free; saltwater pool water must be discharged onto your own property, not into the storm system — Purewater states this as a City of Ottawa requirement, and HB Pools says the same. When the crew lowers your pool, that rule is why the hose runs where it runs.

The freeze science — why this can’t wait

Water expands about 9 percent when it freezes, and in a closed pipe that expansion has nowhere to go — it is the pressure, not the ice itself, that splits the fitting. Ottawa’s January mean daily minimum is −14.3°C (1991–2020 climate normals), and the typical coldest night of the year runs far below that. The parts that burst first are not the straight pipe runs — they’re the rigid housings holding trapped water: skimmer bodies, pump housings, filter tanks and heater heat exchangers. There is no published Ottawa price list for freeze repairs; for scale only, US national guides put filter replacement at US$200–$1,500, heater repair at US$165–$765 and a pump motor at US$700–$1,500. The local arithmetic is simpler: one cracked heater exchanger costs more than a professional closing — published prices span $180 to $599.99 +HST.

DIY or pro?

A proper line blow-out is about air volume, not pressure: the industry tool is a high-volume blower (Cyclone-type, roughly 5 psi at about 130 CFM). A shop compressor is the opposite — high pressure, low flow — and can damage plumbing; a shop vac can clear returns on a simple pool but often can’t bubble a deep main drain. What the pros do that DIYers miss: air-locking the main drain; antifreeze after the blow-out, dosed per line; removing the salt cell and the pressure gauge; storing the plugs in the pump basket; and winterizing the forgotten extras — the second skimmer, the waterfall, the solar loop, the attached spa. Frequently asked questions

When should I close my pool in Ottawa?

Once the water stays consistently below 15°C — typically late September to mid-October here. The published Ottawa booking window runs September 10 to October 20; Ottawa’s average first frost is October 12, and at least one Ottawa firm adds a $100 fee after the first week of November. Closing too early, in warm water, wastes chemicals and grows algae under the cover.

How much does a pool closing cost in Ottawa?

Published prices span $180 to $599.99 +HST: TN Pools starts from $180 (pool type not specified); GBI’s packages run $319.99 to $599.99 +HST; HB Pools is $330 +HST, or $440 with a safety cover; Purewater’s itemized menu builds a typical closing to about $450 before HST; 4 Season’s packages run $225 to $495. Can I close my pool myself?

Yes — if you can move enough air. Lines are cleared with a high-volume, low-pressure blower, not a high-pressure compressor, which can damage plumbing. The steps DIYers most often miss are air-locking the main drain, adding antifreeze after the blow-out, and removing the salt cell and pressure gauge for indoor storage.

Should I drain my hot tub for the winter?

Usually not. If you’ll use it, keep it filled, heated and circulating — that’s the manufacturers’ default advice, and winter is prime soaking season. Drain and winterize it dry only if it will sit unused for months, and follow the manufacturer’s sequence exactly: a tub winterized badly is itself a freeze risk, and freeze damage in a winterized tub is not covered by the manufacturer’s warranty.

Mesh or solid — which winter cover is better?

A solid tarp blocks all light, so it suppresses algae best, but meltwater pools on it and needs a cover pump, and it’s held down with water bags. A mesh safety cover is anchored to the deck, lets water drain through (no pump), and usually lasts longer — but lets in fine silt and some light. Either beats no cover; the anchored safety cover is where most Ottawa pros have landed.
