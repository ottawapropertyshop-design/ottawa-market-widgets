Ontario's mortgage regulator is in the middle of its most aggressive enforcement push in years — and if you are buying a home in Ottawa, renewing a mortgage, or even thinking about a private mortgage, this story is worth two minutes of your time. The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) has proposed shutting down a Markham brokerage whose principal broker allegedly kept arranging mortgages while unlicensed, revoked the licences of 24 brokerages in a single September sweep, and this year imposed one of the largest penalties it has ever handed an individual broker: $600,000.

None of the firms named are Ottawa brokerages. That is not the point. Every mortgage broker your Ottawa neighbours deal with answers to this same regulator, under these same rules — and this crackdown is the clearest map yet of exactly where the system breaks, and how a homebuyer checks that the person handling the biggest transaction of their life is clean. Here is what happened, and the two-minute check that protects you.

The Markham case: operating without a licence (allegations)

On October 15, 2026, FSRA announced a Notice of Proposal — dated September 2, 2026 — to revoke the brokerage licence of Markham-based ZK Financial Inc. and to refuse to issue a broker licence to its principal broker and sole director, Renkai Lin. A hearing has been requested, so everything that follows is what FSRA alleges; none of it has been proven before the Financial Services Tribunal.

FSRA alleges that Lin continued dealing in mortgages while unlicensed — and that between April 29 and June 1, 2026, he took a $2,000 deposit from a borrower and refused to return it. The regulator also alleges false information on licence applications and Annual Information Returns, advance payments demanded on a mortgage of $400,000 or less (which Ontario law restricts), and false or deceptive promotional material. The history matters: in April 2025, Lin and ZK Financial settled an earlier case — Lin paid a $6,000 penalty and accepted a licence condition, and the brokerage's licence was suspended for two months — after a case that began with criminal charges omitted from licence renewals. FSRA's proposal describes what it calls a "pattern of unwillingness to comply," and says new omissions followed the settlement.

The September sweep: 24 brokerages revoked over paperwork

In late September 2026, FSRA revoked the licences of 24 mortgage brokerages across Ontario. This part was not about fraud — it was administrative, and in a way that makes it more instructive, not less. Every brokerage must file an Annual Information Return by March 31; FSRA uses those returns to track what products the industry is selling and where the risks are building. These 24 firms failed to file, repeatedly, despite warnings. FSRA's head of licensing put it plainly: "Compliance with filing requirements is not optional." A brokerage that cannot manage its own regulator filings is telling you something about how it will manage your file.

Fronting and the $600,000 lesson in private lending

Two more cases frame the risks on each side of the desk. In an order issued the same day as the ZK announcement, former broker Paramjeet Kaur settled with a $40,000 penalty. FSRA's findings included "fronting" — allowing herself to be used to facilitate unlicensed dealing — along with false or deceptive documents and failing to verify borrower identities. Fronting is the pattern every borrower should know: the licensed name on the paperwork is not the person actually running your file.

And on March 11, 2026, FSRA imposed six penalties totalling $600,000 on former Guelph broker Claire Drage — among the largest it has ever levied on an individual. The case was uncontested. FSRA found she brokered hundreds of loans that raised more than $100 million from ordinary investors for one developer group, with boilerplate risk warnings instead of property-specific ones, inflated valuations that made loans look safer than they were, and undisclosed conflicts of interest. The developers behind the loans had already sought creditor protection in January 2024. The licence on the door did not protect those investors — the disclosure duties the broker skipped were the protection.

The wider picture

FSRA's first Enforcement Annual Report (fiscal 2024–25) recorded 100 enforcement actions across all the sectors it regulates, up from 65 the year before — and mortgage brokering accounted for the largest share of sanctions, 43 of 80, with roughly $860,000–$875,000 of the approximately $1.2 million in penalties. Ontario has given the regulator real teeth: under the Mortgage Brokerages, Lenders and Administrators Act, an individual faces up to $100,000 per contravention and a brokerage up to $500,000 — ceilings raised in 2022, and, as the Drage case shows, FSRA is now willing to stack them.

What this means if you are buying or renewing in Ottawa

No Ottawa brokerage appears among the 24 revoked firms, and both headline cases are from outside the capital. The Ottawa angle is simpler and more useful: the same registry, the same rules and the same failure patterns apply to every broker in this city. A closed mortgage is safe even if a brokerage later loses its licence — your contract is with the lender, not the broker. Where people get hurt is mid-flight: an application or renewal in progress, an upfront fee paid, or a private or syndicated mortgage arranged through a firm that cuts corners.

The two-minute check: verify any broker before you sign

1. Start with your paperwork — your mortgage documents must show the brokerage's legal name and licence number, and your broker's name. Note the legal name: among September's 24 revoked licences, four were numbered companies, so the name on the office sign may not be the licensed name.
2. Search FSRA's public registry (linked from fsrao.ca) by legal name or licence number. Confirm the status reads Licensed, check the licence type, see which brokerage the individual is authorized under, and look for any conditions on the licence.
3. Check the enforcement record separately in FSRA's enforcement actions database — actions stay posted even if a licence was later reinstated, so a clean current licence does not erase history.
4. Apply the red-flag test: if the person actually handling your file is not the licensed person on the registry — the fronting pattern — or the brokerage name on your documents does not match the registry, stop and call FSRA before signing or paying anything.

Five questions to ask any mortgage broker

1. "Are you licensed, and under which brokerage?" — then verify it yourself in the registry, and check the enforcement database.
2. "How are you paid, and by whom?" — any fee must be disclosed, paid to the brokerage (never cash, never to an individual), and advance deposits are restricted on mortgages of $400,000 or less.
3. "Why is this mortgage suitable for me — in writing?" — ask for the suitability assessment, the total cost of borrowing including all fees, and the material risks, on paper.
4. "Is this a private or alternative mortgage — and what is my exit?" — who the actual lender is, what the fees and renewal terms are, and the realistic path back to a conventional lender.
5. "Who exactly will handle my file?" — if someone other than your licensed broker is gathering your documents and running your application, that is the fronting pattern. Insist on dealing with the licensed person.

A note on who is not in trouble

It is worth saying plainly: the overwhelming majority of Ottawa's mortgage brokers are licensed professionals who do this work properly, and this crackdown is the regulator clearing out the edges of the industry — which is exactly what a regulator is for. The brokers I work with and recommend welcome the registry check; it takes two minutes and it separates them from the operators in this story.
