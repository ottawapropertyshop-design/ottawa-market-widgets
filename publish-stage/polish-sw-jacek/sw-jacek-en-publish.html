St. Hyacinth Polish Parish, Ottawa — History of the Polish Church on LeBreton Street

At the corner of LeBreton Street North and Louisa Street, on the western edge of LeBreton Flats, stands a church that Ottawa's Polish community built with its own hands — literally. St. Hyacinth's Parish (Parafia św. Jacka Odrowąża) is not only the capital's oldest Polish spiritual address. It is also Ottawa Polonia's first great real-estate act: in October 1954, Polish immigrants bought land from the City of Ottawa for a nominal amount — and three years later they were praying in a church of their own. Before the Church: Soldiers, Refugees and the First Polish Mass

The first Holy Mass in Polish in Ottawa was celebrated in November 1942. The parish's real beginning came with the end of the war. Between 1944 and 1949 a large group of Polish soldiers arrived from Great Britain and Italy, together with concentration-camp prisoners and refugees. From their first days in Canada, these men and women wanted one thing: a Polish church and a Polish priest. From 1948, the Missionary Oblates of Mary Immaculate (OMI) provided regular pastoral care. In December 1951, Fr. Jan Sajewicz, OMI — who would become the first pastor — arrived in Ottawa, and Polish Masses were held at St. John the Baptist Chapel at 96 Empress Avenue. The first Parish Committee was formed on January 25, 1952.

October 1954: Polonia Buys Land From the City

Every immigrant community has a moment when it stops being a guest and starts being at home. For Ottawa's Poles, that moment was October 1954. Polish immigrants purchased land at LeBreton and Louisa from the City of Ottawa — for a nominal amount — and formed a Church Construction Committee. The site was blessed on October 7, 1956. On January 28, 1957, Archbishop Marie-Joseph Lemieux officially announced the creation of St. Hyacinth's Parish, and by July 1957 the church was complete. On August 4, 1957, Fr. Sajewicz celebrated the first Mass in the new church. Soldiers and refugees who had arrived in Canada twelve years earlier with a single suitcase now had a church of their own — built less than three years after buying the land.

Visitors Who Mean More Than Visits

The parish's history reads like a short history of postwar Poland itself. On October 16, 1961, General Władysław Anders visited. On September 2, 1969, Cardinal Karol Wojtyła came and brought a gift: relics of St. Hyacinth from Wawel Cathedral — the same relics the future pope carried to Polish parishes across Canada on that tour. In September 1984, during John Paul II's pilgrimage to Ottawa, parishioners met with the Pope. Cardinal Józef Glemp visited on October 7, 1986, and on September 13, 1994 the parish welcomed Lech Wałęsa, President of Poland, and his wife. The church was renewed inside in 1992–1994, a new organ was blessed in 2007 — the parish's 50th jubilee year — and in October 2017 St. Hyacinth's celebrated its 60th anniversary.

1962: The Neighbourhood Disappears, the Church Remains

The hardest chapter of this story is not about the parish at all — it is about what happened to its neighbourhood. On April 18, 1962, the National Capital Commission filed expropriation notices for LeBreton Flats. Some 2,800 residents and hundreds of businesses were displaced; 53 acres were forcibly acquired from 240 landlords for roughly $17 million. The last building — the Duke House — was demolished in October 1965. The entire district east of the church ceased to exist. The church itself, standing on the western edge of the Flats on Louisa Street, survived — five years older than the expropriation that erased its surroundings, and standing there still. The parish defended its place in later years too: in 1989 it bought 27 Louisa Street with parking in mind, and the rezoning fight over that lot was still running in 2011.

St. Hyacinth's Today

The parish at 201 LeBreton Street North is still run by the Missionary Oblates and remains the capital's Polish personal parish. Its groups include the Light-Life Movement (Ruch Światło-Życie), the Living Rosary Circle, the Knights of Our Lady and the Spiritual Adoption movement, and the Oskar Halecki Institute holds lectures in the parish hall. The parish has long run its own building project, known to parishioners as Projekt 2010 and documented in its bulletins. How alive the community was in 2010 comes through in parish council president Roger Furmanczyk's words to the Centretown News that year: "We're the only Polish language parish in the Ottawa-Gatineau region and there are more than 20,000 of us here. We usually have 250 a Mass on Sunday" — a quote from 2010, offered as a witness to its era, not a current statistic.

Polonia in Numbers

Canada (2021 Census): 982,820 people report Polish ethnic or cultural origin — 2.7% of the country. Polish is the mother tongue of 176,010 people in Canada (2021 Census).

Ontario (2021 Census): 461,090 people of Polish origin — the largest provincial total.

Ottawa–Gatineau metropolitan area (CMA), 2021 Census: 37,120 Polish ethnic-origin responses (2.53%).

City of Ottawa, 2021 Census Profile: 30,300 Polish ethnic-origin responses.

City of Ottawa, 2006 Census: 25,685 responses (3.2%).

How to read these numbers: they count ethnic origin, not the language spoken at home; multiple responses were allowed, and the census origin question changed between censuses — so figures from different years and geographies must never be blended or read as a simple growth rate. Ottawa's Polonia will never fit inside a single number, but every one of these tells the truth about its own place and year.

From Wilno to Winnipeg: Polish Parishes Across Canada

St. Hyacinth's is no exception — it is part of the oldest Polish story in Canada. That story begins in 1858, when Kashubian settlers founded Wilno in Renfrew County, Canada's first Polish settlement. The Wilno parish was organized in 1875, and today's Church of Our Lady of Częstochowa, Queen of Poland, dates to 1936. Next door in Barry's Bay, St. Hedwig's was consecrated in 1915; the parish on Karol Wojtyła Square is active today, and its bulletin reported Sunday attendance of 462 in December 2025.


St. Mary's (Our Lady of Częstochowa, Queen of Poland) in Wilno, Ontario. Photo: Hans-Jürgen Hübner, CC BY 3.0, Wikimedia Commons.

Further west, the story is written in great city parishes. Holy Ghost Parish in Winnipeg (1898) opened Canada's first Polish school in 1902 and from 1908 published Gazeta Katolicka, the country's first Polish Catholic newspaper. Toronto gained St. Stanislaus Kostka in 1911, and in 1948 St. Casimir's on Roncesvalles grew out of it — the heart of Toronto's Polonia. In Montreal, the registers of Our Lady of Częstochowa reach back to 1909, and the present church was built in 1946–1947. The newest chapter belongs to the suburbs: St. Maximilian Kolbe in Mississauga was founded in 1979, and St. Eugene's church in Brampton was consecrated only in 2011. The same thread runs almost everywhere on the map: the Missionary Oblates staff or staffed these parishes — the same order that cares for St. Hyacinth's in Ottawa.

What Does This History Have to Do With Houses? Everything.

Notice the pattern: everywhere Poles settled in Canada, they first bought land and built a church — and homes and neighbourhoods grew around it. The Ottawa Polonia of 1954 was not thinking like investors, but that is exactly what they did: they turned a rented place of worship into a permanent community address of their own.
