If your home has an in-ground sprinkler system, October is not the month to get around to its blow out — it is the deadline. Ottawa's first fall frost lands in early to mid October (the Climate Atlas of Canada puts the city's average first frost around October 10), and the first true hard freeze — roughly −2.2°C or colder for a few hours — is what cracks pipes. By November, nights that cold are routine here. A system that still has water in it when that night arrives is a spring repair bill waiting to happen.

The blow out has to happen while the system is still unfrozen, and every irrigation customer in the city needs the same four-to-six-week window — which is why the pros will tell you the real booking month is September. Local companies run winterizations through October and into the first week of November, but after mid-October you are gambling on the forecast.

What a blow out actually is

A blow out is a shut-off, a drain, and a controlled purge of compressed air, zone by zone, until nothing but mist comes out of the heads. Done properly — this follows manufacturer guidance from Rain Bird — it goes like this:

  1. Close the system's mainline shut-off valve so no new water can enter.
  2. Relieve the pressure by running a zone.
  3. Connect a compressor at the blow-out point, through a pressure regulator set to 50 psi — never more.
  4. Blow each zone one at a time, in short cycles of about two minutes or less, repeating until the heads emit only a fine mist.
  5. Leave one zone open while the compressor shuts down, then disconnect.
  6. Set the controller to "Off" or "Rain-Off." Timers can stay plugged in — it preserves your programming. On a pump-fed system, the pump control must be disconnected so the pump can never start dry.
  7. Drain the backflow preventer and leave its ball valves half-open, at 45 degrees, for the winter.
  8. Empty cup-style rain sensors, or tip them so collected water can't sit, freeze and crack the housing.

What breaks if you skip it

Water expands about 9 percent when it freezes, and it expands inside whatever is holding it. The parts fail in a predictable order. First is the backflow preventer — it sits above ground, fully exposed, and a few hours of hard freeze can crack its body; a cracked body usually means full replacement. Next come valves and the manifold, then lateral lines and fittings, especially at low spots that quietly hold water, and finally sprinkler heads and risers. The cruel part: the damage is usually invisible until spring start-up, when zones leak or won't pressurize and the digging begins.

There is no published Ottawa price list for spring freeze repairs. For scale only, US industry price lists put a replacement backflow preventer around US$485 and a manifold rebuild near US$795, with major freeze damage running into the thousands — the local lesson is simply that spring repairs run several times the price of a fall blow-out. One side note: Ottawa's backflow prevention program is aimed at higher-hazard commercial and multi-residential properties. For a typical house, the backflow preventer's winter enemy isn't paperwork — it's ice.

What it costs in Ottawa — and DIY vs. pro

Published local pricing: Yates Custom Lawn Sprinklers in Manotick lists winterization from $109. GreenLawn Sprinkler Systems, also Manotick, lists fall winterization at approx. $180, including a free spring opening. Premier Irrigation & Lighting, based in Richmond, runs blow-outs at about $165. For Ontario context, one GTA company's published zone table runs $100 for 1–4 zones up to $140 for 15–20 zones. In other words: a professional blow-out in Ottawa is roughly a hundred-to-two-hundred-dollar visit, priced by system size.

Can you do it yourself? Yes — with the right compressor, and that is a bigger condition than it sounds. Rain Bird specifies 10–25 CFM of air volume with a hard cap of 50 psi through a regulator. Hunter's limits are 80 psi maximum for PVC pipe and 50 psi for poly. The pros arrive with tow-behind compressors moving 100–185 CFM at regulated pressure; a small shop compressor usually can't move enough air, and a partial blow-out that leaves water in the low spots can be worse than none. The classic DIY mistakes: over-pressurizing and cracking pipe, running a zone dry too long and overheating plastic gear-drive heads, and forgetting to drain the backflow preventer. Compressed air also throws debris — wear eye protection and never stand over a head. Renting a proper tow-behind compressor typically costs about the same as one professional visit, which is why most Ottawa homeowners just book it.

While you're at it — the other October water tasks

Outdoor taps and hose bibs: close the indoor shut-off for each exterior tap, disconnect the hose, open the outdoor tap to drain, open the bleed cap on the indoor shut-off until the dripping stops, and leave the outdoor tap open for the winter. Even a frost-free bib can burst if a hose stays connected. Ottawa plumbers advise having this done by mid-October; a frost-free bib replacement runs $250–$450.

Hoses: drain them fully and store them indoors — trapped water splits them.

Pools: Ottawa-area closings run mid-October (roughly October 10–25), or once the water stays consistently below 18°C.

Frequently asked questions

When is it too late to blow out my sprinklers in Ottawa?

Once a hard freeze — about −2.2°C for a few hours — has already hit a charged system, the damage may be done. Ottawa's first frost arrives in early to mid October and hard freezes are routine by November. Local companies will still winterize into the first week of November, but treat that as the last lifeboat, not the plan. Book in September, blow out in October.

Can I blow out my own sprinkler system?

Yes, if you have a compressor that moves enough air: 10–25 CFM, regulated to no more than 50 psi, each zone blown in short cycles until only mist comes out. Most small home compressors can't do it properly, and renting a tow-behind unit costs about the same as one professional visit. If you go DIY, wear eye protection, never stand over a sprinkler head, and don't forget to drain the backflow preventer and leave its valves at 45 degrees.

What does a sprinkler blow-out cost in Ottawa?

Published Ottawa pricing runs from $109 (Yates, Manotick) to about $165 (Premier Irrigation, Richmond) to approx. $180 including a free spring opening (GreenLawn, Manotick). Sprinkler Solutions by Nutri-Lawn and Irrigation Ottawa quote per system. Larger and rural systems cost more — one Ontario zone table runs $100 for 1–4 zones up to $140 for 15–20 zones.

My system runs off a well. Does anything change?

Yes. On a pump-fed system, drain the pump and remove its drain plugs — a cast-iron pump housing cracks if water is trapped in it — and disconnect the pump control so a power surge can't start the pump dry. The pressure tank and any exposed lines need draining too. Rural well systems are their own service class: Yates, for example, prices out-of-town well systems separately, and above in-town rates.
