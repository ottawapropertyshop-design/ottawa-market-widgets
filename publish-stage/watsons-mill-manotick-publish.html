Watson's Mill in Manotick is older than Canada itself. Raised in 1860 — seven years before Confederation — the limestone mill on the Rideau River still grinds flour today, still anchors its village, and still carries one of Ottawa's most haunting stories. This is the story of Watson's Mill and of Manotick, the village a mill built. Written by Tomasz R. Witek, a REALTOR® who has been selling homes in Ottawa since 2005.

Stand on the bridge at Dickinson Square and you take in the whole story at once: the grey stone mill, the dam spilling white water beside it, and a main-street village that exists because two ambitious men decided this bend of the Rideau River would make them rich. It made something better — it made Manotick.

The Mill That Built a Village

In 1858, Moss Kent Dickinson and Joseph Merrill Currier leased the water-power rights created by a new control dam on the back channel of the Rideau River, where the river splits around Long Island across from the Long Island Locks. Dickinson was a forwarding magnate — the "King of the Rideau," whose steamers and barges ran between Ottawa, Kingston and Montreal — and Currier was a lumberman and foundry owner whose Ottawa workshop cast the mill's own turbines. Together they formed the Long Island Milling Enterprise.

The flour mill they raised in 1860 — first called the Long Island Flouring Mills — was built from locally quarried limestone, with walls over a metre thick at the base. Count it from the river and it rises three and a half storeys; inside, it works across five levels. Around it grew a full industrial complex: a sawmill on the opposite bank (1859), a carding mill (1861), and by 1875 the Canada Bung, Plug and Spile Factory. There was a general store, a cooperage, a carpenter's shop — a village in everything but name.

So Dickinson gave it a name. He called the village Manotick, from an Anishinaabe word meaning "island in the river" — for Long Island, sitting in the Rideau beside the mills. Mill jobs, the store and the post office drew settlers from the fading lock village of Long Island, and by the late 1870s Manotick had grown to about 400 people. Dickinson himself went on to serve as Mayor of Ottawa from 1864 to 1866 and later as a Member of Parliament — but Manotick was his creation, and he is buried in its story on every street.

Still Grinding After All These Years

Most mills of its era are ruins, restaurants or condos. Watson's Mill is still a mill. It is one of the few operating industrial grist mills left in North America, and on Sundays in season (May to October) the water-powered machinery turns again — stone-ground whole-wheat flour is milled in live demonstrations and sold in the mill shop, along with bread baked from it. Some of the original French buhrstone millstones, each weighing around 900 kilograms, are still in use.

The working life was long: Dickinson became sole owner in 1863, the mill stayed in his family until the late 1920s, and in 1946 former manager Harry Watson bought it and gave it the name it carries today — Watson's Mill. Commercial flour grinding finally ended in 1963, after the mill's centenary. The Rideau Valley Conservation Authority bought the mill, Dickinson House and the Carriage Shed in 1972 and restored them, and in 1974 Watson's Mill opened as a museum; Watson's Mill Manotick Inc. took ownership in 2008. In 1979 the square was designated under Part IV of the Ontario Heritage Act — and a point of local pride worth stating plainly: the mill is not a National Historic Site (that honour belongs to the Rideau Canal beside it), but Dickinson Square is the City of Ottawa's only industrial heritage site. Canada Post even put the mill on a stamp in 2010.

Dickinson House and the Ghost of Ann Currier

Across the square stands Dickinson House, built in 1867 as the mill's office, general store and village post office before becoming the Dickinson family home in 1870. Furnished today as it was in the 1870s, it is the gracious half of Dickinson Square. The mill across the yard holds the square's dark chapter.

The documented facts are these. Ann Crosby Currier — Annie — was the second wife of Joseph Currier. They married in January 1861, and about six weeks later, aged just 20, she died inside the mill: her crinoline dress caught in a revolving turbine shaft on the second floor, and she was thrown against a post and killed instantly. The usual telling adds the date — Monday, 11 March 1861 — and the occasion, a small party celebrating the mill's first successful year, on the couple's return from their honeymoon. Joseph never recovered his interest in Manotick; he withdrew from the partnership in 1863, and Ann's grave was later moved to Beechwood Cemetery in Ottawa.

And then there is the legend — and it is best told as legend, because every reputable source does. Visitors and staff have reported a woman in period dress in the second-floor windows, footsteps on the attic stairs, cold spots, an unseen tug at a sleeve. None of it is confirmed; all of it is part of the mill's living folklore, retold on ghost tours and haunted evenings each fall. Believe it or not — a 20-year-old bride died violently in a stone mill in 1861, and Manotick has been telling her story ever since.

What It Feels Like to Live in Manotick

Manotick today is a village wrapped into a city — Ottawa grew out to meet it, and the village kept its own rhythm anyway. Dickinson Square is still its heart, drawing more than 30,000 visitors a year to the mill, the house museum, the used-book sale in the Carriage Shed and Remembrance Park by the dam. The Saturday ritual writes itself: tour the mill, wander the farmers market, coffee on Manotick Main Street, a walk along the river. Through the year the square hosts concerts, outdoor theatre and community gatherings, and every first weekend in June the village celebrates Dickinson Days — parade, fireworks, pancake breakfast and market — timed to Moss Kent Dickinson's June 1 birthday.

People do not move to Manotick by accident. They come for the thing money usually cannot buy back once it is gone: a real village — stone heritage buildings, a working 1860s mill, river air — twenty-five minutes from downtown Ottawa.

Is Watson's Mill still a working mill?

Yes. Watson's Mill (1860) is a working water-powered grist and flour mill and museum — one of the few operating industrial grist mills in North America. In season (May to October) it runs live milling demonstrations on Sundays, and its stone-ground whole-wheat flour is sold on site.

Who was Ann Currier, and is Watson's Mill haunted?

Ann Crosby Currier was the wife of mill co-founder Joseph Currier. She died in the mill in 1861, aged 20, about six weeks after her wedding, when her dress caught in a revolving shaft on the second floor. The haunting — a woman seen in the second-floor windows, footsteps, cold spots — is local legend: often reported, never confirmed, and retold on ghost tours every fall.

When is Dickinson Days in Manotick?

Dickinson Days runs on the first weekend of June each year, timed to founder Moss Kent Dickinson's June 1 birthday — parade, fireworks, pancake breakfast, farmers market and tours of Watson's Mill and Dickinson House in Dickinson Square.

Manotick is a historic Rideau River village inside the City of Ottawa — heritage streets, a working 1860s mill, boutiques and cafés on Manotick Main Street, and river trails, about 25 minutes from downtown.
