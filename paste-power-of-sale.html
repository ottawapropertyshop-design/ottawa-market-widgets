Almost everyone calls it foreclosure. In Ontario that word is usually wrong, and the difference is worth real money — to the homeowner who loses the house, to the lender selling it, and to the buyer trying to purchase it. Ontario law gives a lender two completely different roads when a mortgage goes into default. One is power of sale, the route used for the large majority of lender sales in this province. The other is foreclosure, a court process that is rare here. This guide explains both, step by step, then looks at the numbers behind the current rise in power of sale listings — and at a third seller most buyers have never heard of, the Public Guardian and Trustee, the Ontario government office that sells homes for people who can no longer manage their own affairs.

Power of Sale, Step by Step

A power of sale is the lender's right, written into almost every Ontario mortgage, to sell the property after a default without going to court and without ever taking ownership of it. The process is governed by the Mortgages Act, and it runs like this:

  • Default. The borrower falls behind — usually missed payments. Where a mortgage does not contain its own power of sale, the Mortgages Act supplies a statutory one once the default has continued for three months.

  • Notice of Sale. Once the default has continued for at least 15 days, the lender serves the statutory Notice of Sale on the borrower and on everyone else with a registered interest in the property.

  • The redemption period. The borrower then has at least 35 days — 40 days where the property is a spousal home — to pay the arrears, interest and the lender's costs and stop the process entirely. While that window runs, the Mortgages Act bars the lender from taking other enforcement steps without a court order.

  • Possession and listing. If the borrower does not redeem, the lender takes possession and lists the home on MLS through a listing agent, normally sold "as is" under the lender's own schedule of terms — no warranties, limited or no disclosure, and the lender's choice of closing terms.

  • Sale and accounting. Title passes directly from the borrower to the buyer — the lender never owns the home. The proceeds pay the costs of the sale and the mortgage debt, then any later registered charges. Any surplus goes back to the borrower. If the sale falls short, the lender can sue the borrower for the shortfall.

One protection matters enormously in a power of sale: the lender must take reasonable care to obtain fair market value. A careless sale at an undervalue can be attacked by the borrower as an improvident realization. That duty is why disciplined pricing work — a full comparative market analysis alongside the appraisal, before the home is listed — is the centre of every well-run lender file, and it is why lenders list at the higher of the two values when they differ.

A straightforward power of sale takes roughly three to six months from default to a closed sale.

Foreclosure, the Court Road

Foreclosure is a court action. The lender sues in the Superior Court of Justice under Rule 64 of the Rules of Civil Procedure and asks the court to end the borrower's equity of redemption — the right to pay off the mortgage and keep the property. The court fixes a redemption period; if nobody redeems and nobody obtains an order for sale instead, the court grants a final order of foreclosure and title vests in the lender. From that moment the lender owns the home outright: it can keep it, rent it, or resell it and keep any profit on the resale. The other side of that bargain is that by taking title, the lender generally gives up the right to sue the borrower for any shortfall.

A close relative is the judicial sale, where the court supervises the sale of the property itself and the lender never takes title. Interested parties in a foreclosure action can ask the court for a judicial sale instead — it is the protection used when the property may be worth more than the debt.

Foreclosure is slower — a year or more — and more expensive than a power of sale because of the court steps. Ontario lenders reach for it mainly where there is little or no equity, where title is complicated, or where the file is contested. For the ordinary defaulted mortgage, power of sale is faster, cheaper, and it returns the surplus to the borrower, which is exactly why it is the standard route in this province.

The Numbers: Power of Sale Is Surging

Power of sale is still a small slice of the market — and it is growing fast. According to Valery's analysis of TRREB MLS data published in October 2026, 1,402 homes came on the market as power-of-sale listings across Ontario in the twelve months from October 2025 to September 2026, up 58.4 percent from 885 in the twelve months before. In the GTA the count was 807, up 66.7 percent from 484 — in a year when total new GTA listings actually fell 12.3 percent. On October 28, 2026, 443 power-of-sale homes were for sale across Ontario at the same time.

Ontario homes. Photo: Bardiak, Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

The five-year arc is starker. Toronto's new power-of-sale listings bottomed at roughly 50 a month in 2021 and 2022, when pandemic-era rates made almost every mortgage carryable. By mid-2026 they were running at roughly 480 to 550 a month. The PropTx tracker of active bank and power-of-sale listings, built from real estate board data, counted 94 such listings in June 2023 and 1,551 on November 1, 2026.

No official dollar-volume series for power of sale is published in Ontario, so here is the honest arithmetic from the board counts. Of the 1,402 Ontario power-of-sale listings in the last twelve months, roughly half of the ones that came off the market sold — about 700 homes. The power-of-sale homes offered for sale in late October 2026 carried median asking prices of $1,075,000 for detached houses, $689,000 for townhouses and $529,000 for condos. Blended together, that puts the dollar volume of Ontario power-of-sale transactions in the last twelve months in the range of $550 million — my estimate, built from the board counts and typical power-of-sale prices, not an official figure.

What do these homes actually sell for? In the GTA, power-of-sale homes that sold went for a median 95.6 percent of their last asking price, after a median 32 days on market, against 97.1 percent and 22 days for all sales. A PureProperty analysis of Ontario MLS sales from July 2024 to September 2026 found power-of-sale houses sold 10.8 percent — about $96,000 — below similar homes nearby. Two cautions belong with those numbers. First, only about half of power-of-sale listings that come off the market sell at all; the rest expire or are withdrawn, which is usually a pricing or condition story. Second, these homes are sold as is, with lender terms and limited disclosure — part of any discount is the buyer pricing in real risk.

Ottawa's picture is calmer, and I will not dress it up with Toronto numbers. The Ottawa area saw 38 new power-of-sale listings in the same twelve months, up 11.8 percent from 34 — just 0.16 percent of new listings, one of the lowest shares in the province. Twelve power-of-sale homes were for sale in the city of Ottawa on October 28, 2026, at a median asking price of $819,000, and Ottawa power-of-sale listings that sold achieved 96.5 percent of asking. The wave so far is GTA-led — in Brampton this past summer close to 1 in 50 sales was a power of sale. But the driver is national: more than $200 billion of Canadian mortgages, roughly 1.15 million loans, come up for renewal in 2026, many of them written at pandemic rates. Ottawa's counts are rising from a low base, and the files are coming.

The Third Seller: The Public Guardian and Trustee

There is a third kind of forced sale in Ontario that has nothing to do with mortgage default, and it is the one people are usually reaching for when they talk about "the government selling houses." The office is the Office of the Public Guardian and Trustee — the OPGT — part of Ontario's Ministry of the Attorney General. It is the guardian and trustee of last resort.

Here is how a home ends up in its hands. When an adult is found mentally incapable of managing their property — by a designated capacity assessor, under the Substitute Decisions Act — and that person has no continuing power of attorney and no family member able and willing to act, the law makes the Public Guardian and Trustee their statutory guardian of property automatically. The OPGT then manages that person's money: paying bills, collecting income, protecting assets. When the home must be sold — to fund care, to pay debts, or because the owner will not be returning to it — the OPGT sells it, and the proceeds are held and used for the owner's benefit.

The same office steps in on death. When a person dies and there is no executor and no one else available or eligible to administer the estate, the OPGT administers the estate as a last resort, and a house in that estate is sold the same way. Children's interests run through this office too: the OPGT holds funds in trust for minors, and the Office of the Children's Lawyer protects minors' property interests — which is why a sale touching a minor's interest in an estate property needs the Children's Lawyer's or the OPGT's consent, or a court order, under the Estates Administration Act.

An OPGT sale runs on published rules, and buyers and agents should know them before they write an offer. From the OPGT's own offer instructions:

  • Full MLS exposure first. A property must be listed on MLS for a minimum of eight calendar days before any offer can be presented to the OPGT for consideration.

  • Strictly as is, where is. The OPGT has never lived in the property and makes no representation or warranty about its condition, fitness, zoning or lawful use. The mandatory "as is" clause goes into every offer.

  • A serious deposit. The deposit must be a minimum of 10 percent of the purchase price, certified, held in trust for the OPGT.

  • Five business days, no negotiation. Offers must remain irrevocable for five full business days while the OPGT decides. The OPGT does not counter-offer — it accepts or rejects, and a buyer may submit a fresh offer if rejected. Closings run 30 to 90 days.

The OPGT's listings are administered through TransferEASE, its contracted program administrator, working with local listing brokerages in each community. These are often the most genuinely "as is" sales in the province — homes that may have been lived in for decades, with no one able to speak to their history. For a buyer, the discipline is: inspect before you offer wherever access allows, have financing firmly lined up, and have your lawyer review title early. For the families involved, the point of the system is that a vulnerable person's largest asset is sold on the open market, at a tested price, with the money protected.

Questions and Answers

Is a power of sale the same as a foreclosure?
No. In a power of sale the lender sells the home without taking ownership and without a court order, and any surplus after the debt and costs returns to the borrower. In a foreclosure the lender goes to court, takes title to the property itself, and keeps any later gain. Ontario lenders overwhelmingly use power of sale.

How long does a power of sale take in Ontario?
Roughly three to six months for a straightforward file. The lender can serve a Notice of Sale once the default has continued 15 days, and the borrower then has at least 35 days — 40 for a spousal home — to redeem before the home can be sold.

Can a power of sale be stopped?
Yes, inside the redemption period, by paying the arrears, interest and the lender's costs. Even after that window, a lender may accept a full payout before the sale closes — but every day matters, and a homeowner in this position should speak to the lender and a lawyer immediately.

Do power of sale homes sell below market?
Sometimes, but not automatically. GTA power-of-sale homes that sold in the last year went for a median 95.6 percent of their last asking price, and an Ontario-wide analysis found power-of-sale houses sold about 10.8 percent below similar nearby homes. Against that, buyers take the home as is, with limited disclosure and the lender's terms — part of any discount is payment for real risk.

Who is the Public Guardian and Trustee, and why is it selling houses?
It is the Ontario government office that acts as guardian and trustee of last resort. It manages property for mentally incapable adults who have no one else to act for them, and it administers estates when no one is available to do so. When a home in either situation must be sold, the OPGT sells it on the open market — on MLS, strictly as is — and holds the proceeds for the person or the estate.
